# Responsive Web Visualization 1

## Context: Where

The first web visualization addresses the temporal question of the project:

> **How does the composition of violence change over time?**

The objective is not only to identify when the conflict becomes more or less intense, but to determine **how its form changes from week to week**. In particular, the visualization is designed to reveal periods dominated by direct combat, periods in which one-sided violence becomes more prominent, moments of escalation, and changes in the relative composition of violence. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

For this reason, the data is aggregated at a **weekly level** rather than shown as individual events. Each week becomes a comparable temporal unit containing counts for state-based, non-state, and one-sided violence, together with the corresponding one-sided share and civilian fatalities. The weekly dataset also contains zero-activity weeks explicitly, which prevents gaps in the chart from being confused with missing observations.

The visualization was therefore planned as a coordinated timeline rather than as a single line chart. Three vertically aligned panels allow the user to move from the general structure of the conflict to the specific civilian consequences of one-sided violence while keeping all measures synchronized on the same temporal axis.

## Representation: Timeline

### What it represents

How the conflict's composition changes week to week: the mix of state-based, non-state, and one-sided violence, and the toll of one-sided violence against civilians specifically.

### What it shows

Three time-aligned panels sharing one x-axis (week):

1. **Event composition** - stacked area of `state_based_event_count` / `non_state_event_count` /
   `one_sided_event_count`, in either absolute or composition-percent mode.
2. **One-sided share** - `one_sided_event_share` as a thin area/line.
3. **One-sided civilian fatalities** - `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` as lollipops (stem + dot),
   only drawn for weeks with a non-zero value.

Below the chart, a drag-to-zoom brush strip and a "View as table" fallback (the same rows as a
plain HTML table, for screen readers and no-JS). :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

![web 1 Absolute](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_3/images/Web1.png?raw=TRUE)

Visually, the representation is a **multi-panel temporal visualization** composed of three horizontally aligned charts. The top panel occupies most of the vertical space and represents the weekly composition of conflict events. The middle panel isolates the proportion of events classified as one-sided violence, while the bottom panel emphasizes civilian fatalities from one-sided violence using discrete lollipop marks. Because the panels share the same horizontal scale, a week can be compared vertically across all three measures.

The top panel can be switched between two complementary representations:

- **Absolute events** shows the actual weekly number of state-based, non-state, and one-sided events. This mode is useful for identifying increases and decreases in the overall intensity of recorded conflict activity.
- **Composition %** normalizes each week's events to 100%. This removes the effect of the total number of events and instead emphasizes the changing relative importance of the three categories.

The two modes answer slightly different questions. Absolute mode asks **"how much violence occurred?"**, while composition mode asks **"what kind of violence made up that week's activity?"**. Looking at both avoids confusing a change in proportions with a change in overall intensity.

The second panel provides a more direct reading of this shift by plotting `one_sided_event_share`. A rise in this line indicates that one-sided violence accounts for a larger fraction of the week's recorded events, regardless of whether the total number of events is high or low.

The third panel separates **frequency from human impact**. A week may contain only a limited number of one-sided events but still produce an exceptionally high number of civilian fatalities. This distinction is clearly visible around the annotated **El Fasher massacre** in October 2025: the hovered week shows 33 one-sided events and a one-sided share of 58.9%, while the civilian-fatality panel contains an extreme spike of 58,746 recorded one-sided civilian fatalities.

The visualization is also interactive. The frontend uses the weekly metrics of the currently selected actor, while the conflict-wide view uses the dedicated `__ALL__` series rather than summing actor rows. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

- **Actor scope:** re-renders whenever `selectedActorId` changes (global).
- **Hover:** a shared crosshair line across all three panels; tooltip shows the exact week's
  numbers. Local state only.
- **Click a week:** writes `focusWeek` to the global store - this is what drives the Map into Week
  mode and highlights T0 elsewhere.
- **Drag-to-zoom:** the brush strip below the axis rescales the x-domain of all three panels
  (clamped to the actor's full date range). Entirely local - never touches the shared store, and
  resets available via a "Reset zoom" button that appears only while zoomed.
- **Keyboard:** arrow keys move a virtual cursor over the chart, Enter/Space sets `focusWeek`,
  matching the mouse-click behavior. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

These interactions make the timeline both an analytical visualization on its own and an entry point into the other views: selecting a specific week connects the temporal pattern to its geographic representation in the map.

## Pros and Cons

| **Pro** | **Con** |
|---------|---------|
| The three synchronized panels make it possible to compare **event frequency, composition and civilian consequences** at exactly the same point in time. | Three panels and several metrics make the visualization more complex than a conventional single-series timeline. |
| Switching between **absolute values and percentages** separates overall conflict intensity from changes in violence composition. | Percentage mode can visually exaggerate the importance of a category during weeks with very few total events. |
| Temporal trends, spikes and major discontinuities are immediately visible; extreme episodes such as the October 2025 fatality spike stand out clearly. | Very large fatality outliers compress the scale of the lower panel and can make smaller but still meaningful episodes visually difficult to distinguish. |
| Hover, zoom, actor filtering and linked week selection support detailed exploration without losing the conflict-wide temporal context. | Interactive features such as tooltips and zoom contain information that cannot be communicated completely in a static screenshot. |

## Knowledge: What can we learn from it?

The main analytical goal is to determine whether the Sudan conflict maintains a stable violence profile or whether its composition changes over time. More specifically, the visualization allows us to examine whether weeks dominated by direct armed confrontation are followed, accompanied, or replaced by periods in which one-sided violence accounts for a greater proportion of recorded events.

The distinction between the three panels is important because **event counts and consequences are not equivalent**. A rise in the number of one-sided events tells us that this type of violence is becoming more frequent. A rise in `one_sided_event_share` tells us that it is becoming more important relative to the other forms of violence. A rise in civilian fatalities tells us about severity. The three measures can therefore move together, but they do not have to.

This is difficult to understand from `weekly_metrics.csv` alone. The underlying table stores one row per actor and week, with columns for the different violence categories, fatality measures and one-sided share. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6} A table allows exact values to be retrieved, but it does not make several important structures perceptually obvious:

- prolonged periods in which one type of violence becomes more dominant;
- rapid transitions from one conflict composition to another;
- whether peaks in one-sided violence coincide with broader conflict escalation;
- whether a high one-sided share occurs during a highly active or relatively quiet week;
- whether the number of incidents and their civilian consequences increase together;
- exceptional outliers that are disproportionate to the surrounding weeks.

The timeline converts these values into **position, shape, continuity and aligned temporal patterns**. Instead of reading and comparing hundreds of rows manually, the user can perceive periods, peaks, transitions and co-movements directly.

The absolute and composition modes are particularly useful together. The absolute view preserves the magnitude of conflict activity, while the composition view reveals structural change. For example, a week can have a high proportion of one-sided violence without being one of the most active weeks overall. Conversely, a period of intense conflict may remain predominantly state-based and therefore not represent the same type of shift toward civilian targeting.

The lower fatality panel adds another important piece of knowledge: **the severity of one-sided violence is not necessarily proportional to its frequency**. The October 2025 episode illustrates this clearly. Its exceptional fatality spike would be difficult to understand by looking only at event counts or percentages.

Overall, this visualization provides the temporal foundation for the rest of the project. It identifies **when something changes**, while the following visualizations investigate **where it occurs, which actors are involved, and what happens around major one-sided-violence episodes**. This follows the intended narrative structure of the project: time → space → actors → event-centred analysis. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

## Pipeline from raw information to the visualizations

The timeline reads one derived file, `weekly_metrics.csv`. All analytical work happens upstream in the Python pipeline, while the browser only filters, scales and lays out. 

The whole pipeline runs with `python scripts/build_all.py`.

**What is inside the raw dataset.**

- The UCDP Georeferenced Event Dataset (GED, v26.1) has one row per event: date range (`date_start`, `date_end`), `type_of_violence` (1 state-based, 2 non-state, 3 one-sided), the two sides, fatality estimates (`deaths_a`, `deaths_b`, `deaths_civilians`, `best`/`low`/`high`) and coordinates. The project keeps Sudan events dated 2023-04-15 to 2025-12-31.

**What is inside `weekly_metrics.csv`.** 

- One row per actor × Monday-starting week, including a synthetic conflict-wide actor (`actor_id = "__ALL__"`).

The timeline plots these columns:

| Column | Used for |
|---|---|
| `week_start` | shared x axis |
| `state_based_event_count`, `non_state_event_count`, `one_sided_event_count` | panel 1, stacked area (absolute or percent mode) |
| `one_sided_event_share` | panel 2, share of the week's events |
| `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` | panel 3, lollipops for non-zero weeks |

Weeks without events are stored as `0`, not omitted.

### Steps

1. **Download the raw GED** (v26.1) into `data/raw/GEDEvent_v26_1.csv`, kept unmodified.
2. **`01_clean_events.py`** validates required columns, unique event ids, parseable dates and `date_start <= date_end`, restricts the data to Sudan and 2023-04-15 to 2025-12-31, derives `combatant_fatalities`, `civilian_fatalities` and `one_sided_civilian_fatalities`, and flags (does not drop) events with invalid coordinates. Output: `data/processed/events_clean.parquet`, one row per event.
3. **`02_build_actor_events.py`** expands each event into actor × event rows (a combat event becomes two rows, one per side; a one-sided event becomes one row, the perpetrator). Output: `data/processed/actor_events.parquet` and `actor_lookup.csv`.
4. **`03_build_weekly_metrics.py`** assigns each event to a Monday-starting week by `date_start`, builds a complete weekly calendar with zero-filled gaps, and derives `one_sided_event_share = one_sided_event_count / event_count` (0 for empty weeks). Output: `data/derived/weekly_metrics.csv` (3,289 rows: 143 weeks for `__ALL__` plus one series per actor).
5. **`07_validate_outputs.py`** runs 146 checks (all pass) over every processed and derived file. `build_all.py` runs all stages in order, stops at the first failure, and also writes `metadata.json` (country, analysis period, week convention, H3 resolution, window size).
6. **`npm run sync-data`** (in `web/`, run automatically by `npm run dev` and `npm run build`) copies the five derived files unchanged into `web/.generated/data/`.
7. **At startup** `src/data/loaders.ts` fetches and parses the files through `parsers.ts`, which fails loudly on schema drift and keeps `null` (not observed) distinct from `0`. Views read only through `src/data/selectors.ts`.
8. **`Timeline.ts`** gets the series for the selected actor (`getWeeklyForActor`; `__ALL__` is read straight from the file) and draws three time-aligned SVG panels with D3 scales: stacked event composition, one-sided share, and one-sided civilian fatalities. Stacking and hit-testing live in `timelineMath.ts`.
9. **Interaction** is layered on top: hover shows a shared crosshair and tooltip, drag-to-zoom on a brush strip rescales the x domain (local state only), and clicking a week or pressing Enter writes `focusWeek` to the global store, which puts the map into week mode and marks T0 elsewhere. A 'View as table' fallback renders the same rows as HTML.
