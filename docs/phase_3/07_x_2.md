**Title**: Sudan's war looks different on the ground

**Description**: The frontlines tell only part of Sudan’s story.  
The geography of intense fighting does not fully overlap with the geography of civilian targeting.

In this map, colour represents the share of one-sided violence and opacity the total number of recorded events.

Data: UCDP GED, Apr 2023–Dec 2025.

## Context

This post addresses the spatial question of the project:

> **Does the geography of intense armed conflict coincide with the geography of one-sided violence against civilians?**

The broader web analysis investigates where violence is concentrated across Sudan and whether combat and one-sided violence exhibit the same spatial distribution. The purpose of this social-media version is to condense that question into a single image that can be understood quickly while scrolling through X.

The central distinction is between two spatial properties:

- **how much conflict activity occurred in an area**;
- **what proportion of that activity consisted of one-sided violence**.

These are not equivalent. A location can contain a large number of recorded conflict events while remaining predominantly combat-oriented, while another location with fewer events can have a much larger proportion of one-sided violence.

The visualization was therefore designed to represent **activity and composition simultaneously**, rather than simply plotting all conflict events with the same symbol. This follows the same logic as the interactive geographic visualization, where spatial cells encode both the intensity and composition of recorded violence

For X, the interactive map was transformed into a **static, editorial graphic**. Controls, tooltips, animation and actor filtering were removed and replaced with a clear legend, two annotated examples, and a short explanatory statement. The objective is not to reproduce every function of the web visualization, but to communicate one spatial finding efficiently.

## Representation: Annotated H3 Conflict Map

![X Post 2](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_3/images/X2.png?raw=TRUE)

The main representation is a **thematic point/hexagon map of Sudan** based on spatially aggregated UCDP conflict events.

Each mark corresponds to an **H3 spatial cell** containing recorded conflict activity during the full analysis period. The analytical pipeline assigns events with valid coordinates to H3 cells and aggregates conflict metrics by cell. Events without valid coordinates remain available to the non-spatial analyses but are excluded from this map. 

The post uses two simultaneous visual encodings:

- **Colour → one-sided event share**
- **Opacity → recorded event count**

The colour scale moves from orange toward red:

> **Combat-dominated → One-sided-dominated**

A more reddish cell therefore indicates that a larger share of the events recorded in that area consisted of one-sided violence.

Opacity encodes a different property:

> **Lower activity → Higher activity**

More visually prominent cells correspond to areas containing more recorded conflict events.

This distinction is essential. Colour describes the **composition** of violence, whereas opacity describes its **volume**. The underlying spatial dataset contains both event counts and violence-share metrics specifically to support this separation.

### Annotated examples

Two numbered locations are used to explain how the map should be interpreted.

**1 — High one-sided share**

The first highlighted area contains:

> **17 events · 88% one-sided**

It is not identified as one of the largest concentrations of recorded activity. Instead, it is notable because the overwhelming majority of events represented in that location are classified as one-sided violence.

This is an example of a location that is important because of **violence composition**, rather than simply because of the number of recorded events.

**2 — High activity**

The second highlighted area contains:

> **333 events · 20% one-sided**

This represents a very different profile. It has substantially greater overall recorded activity, while one-sided violence accounts for a much smaller proportion of its events.

Placing these two examples on the same map demonstrates the main visual argument:

> **A major concentration of conflict activity does not necessarily correspond to a major concentration of civilian targeting.**

The comparison would be much less immediate if the post displayed only event counts or only one-sided shares.

### Spatial aggregation

The hexagons are based on H3 aggregation rather than administrative boundaries. Each displayed mark corresponds to a spatial cell, allowing the same geographic unit to be used throughout the study instead of aggregating events into regions of very different shapes and sizes.

In the pipeline, each valid coordinate is assigned to an H3 cell at the configured resolution before events, fatalities and composition metrics are aggregated.

For this social visualization, the cells are displayed at a **uniform visual size at their true cell centres**, as stated in the caption. This makes the graphic cleaner at the reduced dimensions required by X, although it means the visual marks should be interpreted as symbols for H3 cells rather than as exact polygon footprints.

### Geographic and contextual elements

The map deliberately uses a restrained geographic background. Sudan's boundary provides the principal geographic frame, while the data marks remain visually dominant.

The small **before/after satellite image inset of Khartoum's Airport** plays a different role. It does not encode the UCDP variables and should not be interpreted as part of the quantitative map. Instead, it provides contextual imagery connecting the abstract geographic pattern to visible physical destruction associated with the wider conflict.

The left-hand explanatory panel performs the role that interaction would normally play in the web version. Since an X image cannot provide hover tooltips or selectable metrics, the graphic explicitly explains:

- what colour means;
- what opacity means;
- how two contrasting locations should be interpreted;
- the data source and temporal scope.

The result is therefore a **static explanatory visualization**, rather than a direct screenshot of the interactive map.

## Pros and Cons

| **Pro** | **Con** |
|---------|---------|
| Colour and opacity distinguish **violence composition from overall activity**, preventing the two concepts from being conflated. | Encoding two variables in the same marks requires the reader to understand both the colour and opacity legends. |
| The geographic representation immediately reveals **clusters, dispersion and differences between conflict hotspots and one-sided-violence hotspots**. | Aggregating events into H3 cells hides variation between individual events occurring within the same cell. |
| The two numbered callouts provide concrete examples of how a highly active area can differ from an area with a high one-sided share. | A static X post cannot provide the hover values, weekly animation, actor filtering or metric switching available in the web visualization. |
| The simplified layout, short headline and annotations are suited to rapid reading in a social-media feed. | Showing the complete 2023–2025 period collapses temporal change: cells active at very different moments can appear together on the same map. |

## Knowledge: What can we learn from it?

The visualization is designed to examine whether **the geography of armed conflict and the geography of civilian targeting are the same**.

A table containing longitude, latitude, event count and one-sided event share could provide the exact values behind every spatial cell. However, it would be extremely difficult to use such a table to perceive geographic structure.

In tabular form, we would struggle to see:

- whether high-activity locations form geographic clusters;
- whether areas dominated by one-sided violence coincide with those clusters;
- whether civilian-targeting locations are concentrated or dispersed;
- how far apart high-activity and high-one-sided-share areas are;
- whether similar values occur in neighboring cells;
- whether different regions of Sudan exhibit visibly different violence profiles.

The map transforms those numeric coordinates into **spatial relationships**.

Distance, adjacency, clustering and geographic separation become visible immediately.

More importantly, the combined encoding reveals that **high activity and high one-sided share describe different spatial properties**.

The two annotated examples make this distinction explicit:

| Location | Events | One-sided share | Main visual interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **1** | 17 | 88% | Relatively limited activity, but strongly dominated by one-sided violence |
| **2** | 333 | 20% | Major concentration of activity, but predominantly associated with other forms of conflict |

If only event counts were mapped, the first location could appear relatively unimportant.

If only one-sided share were mapped, the second location's much greater volume of conflict activity would be lost.

Showing both allows the viewer to distinguish:

> **Where the war is most active**

from:

> **Where the recorded violence is most strongly oriented toward one-sided violence.**

This is the main knowledge gained from the representation.

The map therefore supports the post's statement that:

> **The geography of intense fighting does not fully overlap with the geography of civilian targeting.**

This should be interpreted descriptively. The representation shows spatial differences in recorded UCDP events during the selected period; it does not by itself explain why those differences occur or establish a causal relationship between combat intensity and civilian targeting.

Compared with the interactive Web Visualization 2, the X version deliberately gives up detailed exploration in exchange for a **single communicative argument**. The web map allows users to change metrics, actors, weeks and dimensionality; this post selects one combination of encodings and two contrasting locations so the spatial distinction can be understood in a few seconds.

## Pipeline from raw information to the visualizations

The post reads one derived file, `h3_weekly_metrics.csv`, plus `metadata.json`. All analytical work happens upstream in the Python pipeline, while the `social/` only filters, scales and lays out.

The whole pipeline runs with `python scripts/build_all.py`.

**What is inside the raw dataset.**

- Each UCDP GED event carries `latitude`, `longitude` and a precision code with its violence type and fatality estimates;
- Events with missing or invalid coordinates are kept in the cleaned data but flagged `spatial_eligible = False` and are excluded from this map.

**What is inside `h3_weekly_metrics.csv`.**

- One row per actor × week × H3 cell, plus conflict-wide `__ALL__` rows.

This post uses only the `__ALL__` rows:

| Column | Used for |
|---|---|
| `h3_id`, `h3_center_lat`, `h3_center_lon` | cell identity and exact position (never recomputed) |
| `event_count` | opacity (intensity) and the activity floor |
| `one_sided_event_count` | numerator of the one-sided share |
| `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` | picking the highest-toll cell |

The `__ALL__` rows come from the unique event table, never from summing actor rows.

### Steps

1. **Download the raw GED** (v26.1) into `data/raw/GEDEvent_v26_1.csv`, kept unmodified.
2. **`01_clean_events.py`** validates required columns, unique event ids, parseable dates and `date_start <= date_end`, restricts the data to Sudan and 2023-04-15 to 2025-12-31, derives `combatant_fatalities`, `civilian_fatalities` and `one_sided_civilian_fatalities`, and flags (does not drop) events with invalid coordinates as `spatial_eligible = False`. Output: `data/processed/events_clean.parquet`, one row per event.
3. **`02_build_actor_events.py`** expands each event into actor × event rows (a combat event becomes two rows, one per side; a one-sided event becomes one row, the perpetrator). Output: `data/processed/actor_events.parquet` and `actor_lookup.csv`.
4. **`04_build_h3_metrics.py`** keeps only `spatial_eligible` events, converts each coordinate to an H3 cell at resolution 5, validates the ids, and aggregates by week, cell and actor. Output: `data/derived/h3_weekly_metrics.csv`.
5. **`07_validate_outputs.py`** runs 146 checks (all pass) over every processed and derived file. `build_all.py` runs all stages in order, stops at the first failure, and also writes `metadata.json` (country, analysis period, week convention, H3 resolution, window size).
6. **`npm run sync-data`** (in `social/`) copies the derived CSVs and `metadata.json` unchanged into `social/.generated/data/`.
7. **`x/post-02-geography/main.ts`** keeps the `__ALL__` rows, sums `event_count`, `one_sided_event_count` and `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` per cell over the whole period, and recomputes the one-sided share from the summed counts (never an average of weekly shares).

   It projects the cell centers with `d3-geo` (Mercator, fitted to the map area) over the Sudan outline from `world-atlas` (Natural Earth, 50 m) and draws each cell as a uniform-size hexagon at its true position. Fill color runs from combat orange to one-sided red on the share; opacity (0.4 to 1, square-root scale) encodes `event_count`.

   Two callouts follow a fixed rule: the cell with the highest one-sided share among cells at or above the 90th percentile of `event_count`, and the cell with the largest one-sided civilian toll (skipped if it is the same cell). Cells are identified only by their values, never by an invented place name.
8. **`npm run export:x`** builds the pages with Vite, renders each at 1600 × 900 px with Playwright and writes `social/output/x-02-geography.png`.

This is not a screenshot of the web map: there is no basemap, zoom, week stepper or 3D extrusion, and hexagons are drawn at a normalized size (position exact, size not the literal H3 boundary) so a dense grid stays legible.
