# Responsive Web Visualization 4

## Context: Before & After

The fourth visualization addresses the most analytical question of the project:

> **What happens in the weeks immediately before and after major episodes of one-sided violence against civilians?**

While the previous visualizations examine **when**, **where**, and **who**, this view reorganizes time around major episodes of civilian violence. Instead of comparing actors on ordinary calendar dates, each episode is aligned around a common reference point:

$$
T0 = \text{week of a selected peak in one-sided civilian fatalities}
$$

The surrounding weeks are then represented relative to that event, normally as:

$$
T-8,\ldots,T-1 \mid T0 \mid T+1,\ldots,T+8
$$

This makes episodes occurring at very different points in the Sudan conflict directly comparable. 

The objective is to examine whether major episodes of one-sided violence occur in recognizable **temporal contexts**: for example, whether they are surrounded by elevated combat activity, combatant fatalities, or actor losses. The visualization is descriptive rather than causal. Similar temporal patterns can reveal associations, but they cannot demonstrate that preceding combat activity caused later violence against civilians. 

Episodes are also not manually chosen in the browser. They are detected upstream for each actor from peaks in `one_sided_civilian_fatalities`, ranked by magnitude and subjected to a minimum temporal separation rule. This keeps the analysis reproducible rather than selecting visually interesting cases after seeing the results.

## Representation: Event-centred Temporal Analysis ("04 / BEFORE & AFTER")

### What it represents

What combat activity and fatalities look like in the weeks immediately before and after an actor's major episodes of one-sided violence against civilians.

Instead of representing ordinary chronological time, the visualization uses **relative time**, with every episode aligned around its own T0. This allows patterns surrounding multiple episodes and actors to be compared on the same temporal scale.

### What it shows

The visualization uses a two-column desktop layout.

The larger left section contains:

1. a **background metric selector**;
2. a **summary line and interquartile band**;
3. an **episode-by-relative-week matrix**.

The right section contains a persistent **Episode Details** panel for the episode currently hovered or selected.

### Summary chart

The upper part of the visualization aggregates the episodes currently displayed.

The x-axis is **relative week**, extending from `T-8` to `T+8`, while the y-axis represents the selected background variable. A dashed vertical line marks **T0**.

The orange line represents the **median** value of that background metric at each relative week, while the surrounding shaded region represents the **interquartile range (IQR)**.

The user can choose between:

* **Combat events**
* **Combatant fatalities**
* **Actor deaths suffered**

Changing this control updates the summary chart, the matrix background cells, and the corresponding label in the Episode Details panel. 

This summary answers a question such as:

> Across the episodes currently displayed, what does the typical level of combat activity look like before and after T0?

Using the median and IQR rather than a simple mean is particularly useful because conflict measures can be extremely skewed by a few very large episodes.

In the first screenshot, for example, **combat events** are selected. The summary indicates the typical combat-event count and its dispersion from eight weeks before to eight weeks after the selected one-sided-violence episodes.

In the second screenshot, the view is restricted to **SFA** and the selected background measure is **combatant fatalities**, allowing the temporal context of that actor's own episodes to be studied separately.

### Episode matrix

Below the summary is the main event-centred matrix.

Each:

* **row** = one detected episode;
* **column** = one relative week from T-8 to T+8.

The central T0 column is visually emphasized with a vertical band and reference line, making the moment of peak one-sided civilian violence immediately identifiable across every row. 

The matrix uses two encodings at the same time.

The **background colour of the cell** represents the currently selected military-context variable:

* combat events;
* combatant fatalities; or
* actor deaths suffered.

Darker cells indicate larger values of the selected metric.

A **red circle** is superimposed on the cell whenever one-sided civilian fatalities are recorded. The size of the circle represents the number of those fatalities. Its radius is constrained so that small non-zero values remain visible while extreme values do not completely cover the cell. 

The matrix therefore visually separates:

> **military context = cell background**

from

> **one-sided civilian toll = red circle**

This makes it possible to compare the timing of civilian violence with several indicators of surrounding armed conflict without collapsing them into one metric.

### Observed zero vs. unavailable data

The matrix also explicitly distinguishes two cases that could otherwise look identical:

* **0 (observed):** the week exists within the analysis period but no activity was recorded for that metric;
* **Not observed:** the relative week falls outside the available analysis period.

Not-observed cells use a diagonal hatch rather than the normal colour scale.

This distinction also affects the summary statistics: unavailable weeks are excluded rather than interpreted as zeros. 

The underlying pipeline follows the same rule. Weeks inside the analysis period with no activity receive genuine zero values, whereas weeks outside the observation period remain missing and are marked `is_observed_week = False`. 

### Episode details and tooltip

Hovering a row or cell exposes the exact values behind the visual encodings.

The tooltip reports:

* actor and episode;
* episode rank;
* T0;
* relative week;
* calendar week;
* whether that week was observed;
* combat events;
* combatant fatalities;
* actor deaths suffered;
* one-sided events;
* one-sided civilian fatalities;
* total civilian fatalities.

The persistent panel on the right instead summarizes the selected episode, including its T0 date, peak value, metrics at T0 and number of observed weeks. 

The first screenshot, for example, highlights an **SFA episode with T0 on 20 October 2025**. Its peak value is 58,746 one-sided civilian fatalities and the interface annotates the week as coinciding with the **El Fasher massacre**. At T0 the panel reports 23 combat events, 89 combatant fatalities, 11 actor deaths suffered and 33 one-sided events.

The episode has all **17 of 17 weeks observed**, so the complete T-8 to T+8 period can be interpreted without boundary-related missing weeks.

## Interaction

The visualization supports several forms of exploration.

* **Background metric:** switches between combat events, combatant fatalities and actor deaths suffered.
* **Actor scope:** follows the globally selected actor. A specific actor shows that actor's episodes, while the all-actors view displays the strongest conflict-wide episode set used for presentation.
* **Hover row or cell:** updates the Episode Details panel and shows a detailed tooltip for the corresponding relative week.
* **Click row or cell:** selects both the episode's actor and its T0 week globally.
* **Cross-view connection:** clicking an episode causes the Timeline to focus on T0, the Map to enter Week mode at T0, and the Actors visualization to select the corresponding actor. 

The visualization therefore works not only as the final analytical view but also as a way to return from an episode-level observation to its temporal, geographic, and actor context elsewhere in the website.

## Pros and Cons

| **Pro**                                                                                                                                                            | **Con**                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Aligning all episodes around **T0** makes events from different calendar dates directly comparable and exposes recurring before/after patterns.                    | Relative time deliberately removes ordinary calendar chronology, so historical sequence is less immediately visible without the tooltip or details panel. |
| Combining **cell intensity with fatality circles** allows military context and civilian consequences to be examined simultaneously.                                | Two encodings inside every matrix cell increase visual complexity, especially when many episodes are displayed.                                           |
| The summary median and IQR provide a compact view of the pattern across multiple episodes without allowing a single extreme observation to define the whole trend. | Aggregating episodes into a median can hide heterogeneous behavior: individual episodes may differ substantially from the summary pattern.                |
| Explicitly distinguishing **observed zero from not observed** prevents unavailable weeks from being misinterpreted as an absence of conflict.                      | Episodes close to the start or end of the dataset can still have incomplete temporal windows, reducing their comparability with fully observed episodes.  |

## Knowledge: What can we learn from it?

The central analytical question is whether major episodes of one-sided civilian violence occur in isolation or whether they are associated with recognizable patterns in the surrounding conflict environment.

The visualization allows us to ask:

* Does combat activity tend to rise before T0?
* Are combatant fatalities elevated immediately before or after major civilian-violence episodes?
* Do actors experience greater losses around these episodes?
* Is T0 usually part of a broader period of intense conflict, or does it often appear as a more isolated event?
* Do different actors show similar or different temporal patterns?
* Are patterns visible across multiple episodes, or are they driven by a small number of exceptional cases?

A conventional calendar-based table makes these questions difficult to answer because each episode occurs on a different date. For example, an SFA episode in October 2025 and an RSF episode in June 2023 would occupy entirely different regions of a normal time series.

The event-window transformation makes them comparable by replacing calendar time with:

$$
\text{relative week}
=
\text{calendar week} - T0
$$

Every episode therefore shares the same analytical structure:

$$
T-8 \rightarrow T-1 \rightarrow T0 \rightarrow T+1 \rightarrow T+8
$$

The resulting dataset contains one row for each **episode × relative week** combination and carries both the episode metadata and surrounding conflict metrics. 

A raw table could provide all of those values, but it would not readily reveal:

* whether darker military-context cells cluster before or after T0;
* whether large civilian-fatality circles coincide with periods of elevated combat;
* whether several episodes exhibit similar temporal shapes;
* whether individual actors behave differently from the overall episode set;
* whether apparent patterns are consistent or driven by isolated episodes;
* whether missing portions of an episode window result from the dataset boundary rather than zero activity.

The matrix makes these patterns perceptually visible through **alignment**. Once every episode shares the same reference column, the viewer can scan vertically around T0 rather than manually matching unrelated calendar dates.

The summary chart adds a second level of analysis. Instead of asking only what happened during one episode, it asks what the **typical temporal profile across the displayed episodes** looks like. The median line shows the central tendency, while the interquartile band indicates how consistent or heterogeneous that pattern is across episodes.

This distinction is important. A peak or increase visible in a single row is an episode-specific observation. A similar feature appearing in the median profile suggests that the pattern occurs across multiple displayed episodes. Neither, however, establishes a causal relationship.

The visualization therefore supports statements such as:

> **Combat activity was elevated around several major episodes of one-sided violence.**

or:

> **The episodes display substantial heterogeneity in their surrounding military context.**

It does **not** by itself support statements such as:

> **Military losses caused the actor to target civilians.**

The implementation deliberately preserves this distinction by using descriptive language such as *before*, *after*, *surrounds*, and *associated with*, rather than causal terminology. 

Overall, Web Visualization 4 completes the analytical progression of the project:

> **WHEN?** identifies temporal changes
> **WHERE?** locates them geographically
> **WHO?** compares the actors involved
> **BEFORE & AFTER?** examines the temporal context surrounding the most important episodes

It is therefore the view that most directly connects the initial research question about shifts toward civilian targeting with observable patterns in the conflict data, while keeping the conclusions explicitly descriptive rather than causal.


## Pipeline from raw information to the visualizations

The before/after view reads one derived file, `event_windows.csv`, plus `metadata.json` for the window size. All analytical work happens upstream in the Python pipeline, while the browser only filters, scales and lays out.

The whole pipeline runs with `python scripts/build_all.py`.

**What is inside the raw dataset.**

- Each UCDP GED event has a date range, a violence type, its sides and fatality estimates, from which the pipeline derives combat activity, actor losses and `one_sided_civilian_fatalities`.

**What is inside `event_windows.csv`.**

- One row per episode × relative week (459 rows, 27 episodes from 4 actors, window T-8 to T+8).

The view plots these columns:

| Column | Used for |
|---|---|
| `episode_id`, `actor_name`, `t0_date`, `peak_rank`, `peak_metric_value` | row labels and the Episode Details panel |
| `relative_week`, `calendar_week` | matrix columns (T-8 ... T+8) and tooltips |
| `is_observed_week` | tells observed zeros apart from weeks outside the analysis period |
| `combat_event_count`, `combatant_fatalities`, `actor_deaths_suffered` | background metric: matrix cell color and median band |
| `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` | red circle size at each cell |

Weeks inside the analysis period with no events are `0` and `is_observed_week = True`. Weeks outside it are left missing (`null`) with `is_observed_week = False`, and the view never renders them like zero.

### Steps

1. **Download the raw GED** (v26.1) into `data/raw/GEDEvent_v26_1.csv`, kept unmodified.
2. **`01_clean_events.py`** validates required columns, unique event ids, parseable dates and `date_start <= date_end`, restricts the data to Sudan and 2023-04-15 to 2025-12-31, derives `combatant_fatalities`, `civilian_fatalities` and `one_sided_civilian_fatalities`, and flags (does not drop) events with invalid coordinates. Output: `data/processed/events_clean.parquet`, one row per event.
3. **`02_build_actor_events.py`** expands each event into actor × event rows (a combat event becomes two rows, one per side; a one-sided event becomes one row, the perpetrator). Output: `data/processed/actor_events.parquet` and `actor_lookup.csv`.
4. **`03_build_weekly_metrics.py`** builds the zero-filled weekly series per actor (`weekly_metrics.csv`). **`05_build_actor_profiles.py`** builds `actor_profiles.csv`.
5. **`06_build_event_windows.py`** takes each actor's series of `one_sided_civilian_fatalities` and finds local maxima (plateau ties go to the earliest week). It ranks them by magnitude, keeps a peak only if it is at least `min_gap_weeks` (6) from those already kept, and stops at `top_k_per_actor` (10). The peak week is T0. It then writes 8 weeks before and after each T0, with episode ids of the form `<actor_id>__<YYYY-MM-DD>`. `__ALL__` is excluded from detection. Output: `data/derived/event_windows.csv`.
6. **`07_validate_outputs.py`** runs 146 checks (all pass) over every processed and derived file. `build_all.py` runs all stages in order, stops at the first failure, and also writes `metadata.json` (country, analysis period, week convention, H3 resolution, window size).
7. **`npm run sync-data`** (in `web/`, run automatically by `npm run dev` and `npm run build`) copies the five derived files unchanged into `web/.generated/data/`.
8. **At startup** `src/data/loaders.ts` fetches and parses the files through `parsers.ts`, which fails loudly on schema drift and keeps `null` (not observed) distinct from `0`. Views read only through `src/data/selectors.ts`.
9. **`eventWindowMath.ts`** groups rows into episodes, computes the per-week median and interquartile range (unobserved weeks are left out of the statistic, not counted as zero), maps values to a sequential color (`null` stays `null`) and sizes the circles (square-root scale with a floor and a cap). For `__ALL__`, `getEpisodesForActor` shows the top 20 episodes by peak magnitude, a display-only filter.
10. **`EventWindowMatrix.ts`** draws the summary (median line and IQR band, T0 marked) above the matrix (one row per episode, one column per relative week, hatched cells for not observed) beside a persistent Episode Details panel. A selector switches the background metric.
11. **Interaction:** hovering a row or cell updates the details panel only; clicking writes both `selectedActorId` and `focusWeek = t0_date` to the global store, so the timeline jumps to T0, the map enters week mode at T0 and the actor is highlighted. The wording stays descriptive: temporal association, never causation.
