## Context: Seeing the Damage of War

The project documents the physical effects of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine: damage to residential buildings, infrastructure, streets, monuments, and public spaces. People can view the material online, through VR headsets, and in travelling exhibitions.

Here is an example of what we can see in the exhibit:

![VR Gif](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_2/gifs/Alt2.gif?raw=TRUE)

It focuses on the view from inside damaged areas, with the belief that recording those views preserves evidence for memory and possible reconstruction.

## Representations: Immersive VR and AR

First of all we encounter the street-view style of hotspots for destruction in Ukraine. The visualization app in embedded on the website as a "taste" of what the full experience can look like.

![VR](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_2/images/Alt2VR.png?raw=TRUE)

This **immersive spatial representation** allows the audience to put themselves around an interactive 360-degree panorama. What immediately catches the eye is the freedom that comes with this visualization: we are in the middle of a devastated area, usually inacessible and dangerous, and we can look in different directions by moving our head or pointer to check/explore the state of the area. If this data remained in tabular format it would not be possible to translate the news of bombings into an almost palpable reality. This communication breaks the barrier of our war-blind reality and obliges the viewer to immediately recognize the destructive consequences of this conflict.

Another core element of the panoramas is represented by how rooms, streets, vehicles, debris, and neighbouring buildings connect. This allows the user to understand how the conflict has spread around these (mostly residential areas). Also, the absence of sound-related representations, adds a serious vibe to the experience.

The user can also switch between positions by changing hotspots or tour links.

There is also another kind of representation, given by **laser-scanned 3D models**.

![AR](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_2/images/Alt2AR.png?raw=TRUE)

The website states that these are also used by the government to assess the amount of damage and plan for restoration.

Each view shows a different story, as we see damaged infrastructures, means of transport (cars, airplanes) and even military vehicles. This adds to the "ghost town" view.

Last but not least, we have to consider that the full experience is set in an exhibition. Therefore, using a headset we are able to make the experience more physical.

## Knowledge: What can we learn from it?

Immersion can make the emotional impact stronger.

A table can record a location, coordinates, date, building type, and damage category. The immersive view shows **how the damage is arranged**. Viewers can see whether it affects one building or continues, how debris fills streets, and how damaged structures relate to surrounding infrastructures.

Moving between sky and street views helps with orientation and scale:

1. From above, viewers can take in an affected neighbourhood;
2. Within a panorama, they see its walls, rooms, and roads;
3. A label such as "residential building destroyed" becomes easier to understand as a place.

The **views also preserve details that a damage category leaves out**: explosion direction, exposed interiors, and which damaged/standing elements remain alongside one another. These observations can preserve testimony, help public understanding, and make assessments of the structures.

Overall, the project is mainly for the international public, including people far from Ukraine. It lets them look around documented places as active observers.

## Pipeline from raw information to the visualizations

Unfortunately we initially had **no description** of how the visualization was made. However, the website states the following in their [About Us](https://warupclose.com/about-us/) section:

"The project is initiated by experts from Discover.ua and FreegenGroup. Mykola Omelchenko, a photojournalist with years of experience and the first Google Trusted Photographer in Ukraine, has embarked on photo shooting for the War up Close.

Using **state-of-the-art technologies, we capture the damage inflicted by the aggressor and put the photos on Google Maps, social media pages, and the project website**. For better immersing and a more realistic experience, **we show footage of Russian crimes with the help of VR goggles and immersive exhibitions**. Besides, a number of virtual museums of war has been created in Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Kharkiv regions in coordination with public agencies for tourism. [...]

The War up Close Project team is in close cooperation with the State Emergency Service, the Ministry of Internal Affairs, the Ministry of Culture and Information Policy of Ukraine and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Ukraine. Our **panoramic views and drone footages assist rescuers in debris removal and examining the actual scope of damage**. We also specialise in **3D modelling of monuments and historic buildings**.

Despite the dangers, we are capturing crimes against Ukraine and humanity for all the future generations to preserve the memory of this tragedy."

Then, we found this description at [The War up Close Project – memorialization of destructions](https://onova.org.ua/en/projects/the-war-up-close-project):

"The Discover team have gone through several stages to create the VR museum:

1. Developing a detailed concept of the project, i.e. its general vision, idea and plan.
2. Obtaining permits for photo and video shooting from competent public authorities and agencies.
3. Conducting the shooting at great personal risk, as obtaining detailed footage often required us to come very close to destroyed and dangerous buildings.
4. Designing and programming the VR museum section.
5. Creating Google Street Views of the tours and adding them to Google Maps.
6. Spreading the word about the museum in mass media."
