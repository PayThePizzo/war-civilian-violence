## Context: Russian Bombings in Ukraine

By 2025, Russian drone and missile attacks had made sirens and explosions recurring sounds of life in Ukraine. Alone, displaying the amount of drones, missiles and alarm durations are harder to understand. This is why introducing noise is crucial to understand what repeated nighttime attacks sound and feel like.

The Washington Post combines data with recordings from the field and audio that plays as their readers scroll. Here, **sound is part of the evidence**, which places this example among the alternative approaches. Thanks to this the readers receive an immersive experience, by being able to both hear the environment of an attack and use the charts to verify how often attacks occurred.

---

## Representations: Sound and Charts

The experience asks readers to turn on the sound and listen as they scroll.

![Sounds](https://github.com/PayThePizzo/war-civilian-violence/blob/main/docs/phase_2/images/Alt1Sounds.png?raw=TRUE)

Recorded explosions, drones, and warning sirens accompany the story and graphics, including:

- Drone launches from January 2024 to July 2025;
- Daily report of drones launched, shot down, and jammed;
- Comparisons between the perceived loudness of war sounds and familiar everyday sounds;
- Timelines showing how many hours air-raid sirens lasted during the day and at night;
- Photographs, maps, and testimony that connect the sounds to the places where people live.

This is an **interactive sonic or aural data narrative**. It combines quantitative graphics with documentary recordings.

The way these charts are made seem to communicate a sense of "endlessness" with respect to the attacks and sirens.

The story moves between three scales:

1. Charts covering several months show the escalation;
2. Daily graphics distinguish launches from interceptions and jamming;
3. Sound and testimony bring readers back to one night and its duration.

Scrolling keeps these layers together, and an audio control lets readers choose when to listen.

Overall, this is made for an international audience on both desktop and mobile. While readers take part by scrolling and listening, they can use the charts to have a numerical comparison. The presence of the audio and images makes it easier for the viewer to empathize with Ukranian civilians.

## Knowledge: What can we learn from it?

The daily graphics separate "launched," "intercepted," and "reached the defended area," **helping readers distinguish the severity of an attack**.

The siren timelines show **how much time alarms take up**. This is important because people may spend hours uncertain whether an alarm will end quietly or with an impact. Here, showing both day and night differences helps us understand how this environment may cause multiple disruption to sleep and ordinary routines. As a consequence, this **helps the audience perceive** (through the concept of sleep), the **level of stress civilians live every day**.

Hearing the sounds adds something a table cannot provide. Loudness, repetitive sirens, and sudden explosions give a sense of distance and threat. Plus, pairing them with an empirical reference, keep the users grounded into the experience.

Taken together, the charts and recordings suggest that the attacks are becoming more frequent, occupying more time of the night, and repeatedly putting civilians on alert. This **helps readers understand the escalation as an experience of daily life**.
